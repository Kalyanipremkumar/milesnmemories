    Shrinathji — When a Journey Became a Home

    How a Krishna image from Govardhan found a new home in Mewar, and how devotion there gave rise to the art of Pichwai painting.

    Sometimes, a place becomes famous not because a king founded a city, but because people believe the divine chose to stay there.

    Where and when

    The story of Nathdwara begins far away, at Govardhan Hill near Mathura.

    It begins in Braj, the Krishna country around Mathura in present-day Uttar Pradesh, and ends in Mewar, in southern Rajasthan, on the banks of the Banas river. Before Shrinathji came, the place was a village called Sihad (also written Sinhad). The temple is known as Shrinathji ki Haveli, the "mansion" of Shrinathji, and is run like a household.

    The people

    Shrinathji represents Lord Krishna as a young boy lifting Mount Govardhan to protect the people of Braj from torrential rain. His raised left arm recalls that beloved episode from Krishna's childhood.

    Shrinathji is especially important in the Pushtimarg tradition founded by Vallabhacharya, where devotion is expressed through loving service, known as seva.

    Vallabhacharya (usually dated 1479–1531) founded the Pushtimarg; Britannica records that he "discovered" the image at the foot of Govardhan. His son Vitthalnath is credited with shaping the elaborate daily seva that still structures worship of Shrinathji. Maharana Raj Singh of Mewar was the ruler who received the image in his kingdom.

    What happened

    During the seventeenth century, amid threats to temples in the Mathura region under Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, Shrinathji's image was moved from Govardhan in search of safety.

    In 1669–70 major temples were demolished on Aurangzeb's orders, including the Keshava Deva temple at Mathura. The temple's own account and museum catalogues agree that the image was moved to keep it safe. Historians still debate how far Aurangzeb's temple policy was driven by religion and how far by politics, and much of the detail of the journey comes to us through the community's own tradition.

    After a long journey, the image reached Mewar, where Maharana Raj Singh offered protection.

    Today, the temple is unlike many others. Shrinathji is cared for with the affection given to a beloved child. He is awakened, dressed, offered food and honoured through different darshans during the day.

    This devotion also gave rise to a remarkable artistic tradition.

    The beautiful painted cloth backdrops displayed behind Shrinathji are called Pichwai paintings. They portray lotuses, cows, peacocks, festivals, seasons and scenes from Krishna's life.

    Pichwais were changed daily, seasonally and for festivals: lotus ponds in summer, peacocks and dark clouds in the monsoon, brocades in winter. Families of painters settled in Nathdwara, and painters still work in the lane known as Chitron ki Gali, the street of paintings.

    For someone who loves traditional Indian painting, Nathdwara is therefore more than a pilgrimage destination. It is a place where devotion and art have grown together for centuries.

    Legends and debates

    And here comes the story that devotees continue to tell.

    As the cart carrying Shrinathji passed through the village of Sihad, its wheels became stuck in the ground. Despite repeated attempts, the cart would not move.

    The devotees interpreted this as a divine sign. Shrinathji had chosen his new home.

    A shrine was established there in 1672, and the settlement became known as Nathdwara — the gateway to the Lord.

    Which year? 1672 is the most commonly given date (the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the temple's own account), but the Victoria and Albert Museum gives 1671 and some sources give 1670.

    Wisdom to carry forward

    A home is not always the place where a journey begins. Sometimes, it is the place where one finally finds belonging.

    The journey of Shrinathji is remembered as a story of protection, faith and welcome.

    It also reminds us how one sacred tradition can shape an entire town — its art, music, food, festivals and everyday life.

    My connection

    We visited Shrinathji Temple at Nathdwara on the morning of 18 October, after leaving Kumbhalgarh at about 7:30, and before going on to Eklingji and Udaipur. In my diary I noted that the town is associated with Pichwai paintings, devotional works that depict Shrinathji and seasonal or festival settings, and that this connection between worship and painting naturally interested me. As I wrote then, "In Rajasthan, art so often belongs not only in galleries, but also in the rhythms of devotion."

    The next day in Udaipur I met artists who work in the miniature-painting tradition and learned about their processes, including how colours are prepared and how brushes are made. They were not Pichwai painters, but the conversation brought me closer to the kind of patient, inherited skill that this story is about.
