Shrinathji and the Paintings That Hang Behind God

How a Krishna image from Braj found a home in Mewar, and how the painted cloths that hang behind it became one of India's great devotional arts.

In the temple town of Nathdwara, about 48 kilometres north-east of Udaipur, Krishna is not treated as a distant god. He is treated as a much-loved child who lives at home. He is woken, dressed, fed, entertained and put to bed, and his doors open to devotees only at fixed moments through the day.

The image at the centre of this life is Shrinathji: Krishna as a young boy, carved in dark stone, his left arm raised to lift Mount Govardhan. Behind him hangs a painted cloth that changes with the seasons and festivals. These cloths are called pichwai, and they grew into an art form of their own.

Where and when

The story begins in Braj, the Krishna country around Mathura in present-day Uttar Pradesh, and ends in Mewar, in southern Rajasthan. It runs from the late fifteenth century to the late seventeenth.

Nathdwara stands on the banks of the Banas river. Its name is usually explained as "gateway of the Lord". Before Shrinathji came, it was a village called Sihad or Sinhad. The temple is known as Shrinathji ki Haveli, the "mansion" of Shrinathji, and is run like a household.

The people

Vallabhacharya (usually dated 1479–1531) was a philosopher and teacher who founded the Pushtimarg. Britannica translates the name as "way of flourishing"; the Victoria and Albert Museum calls it the "way of grace". Its central idea is that devotion is nourished by divine grace.

Vitthalnath, Vallabha's son, succeeded him as head of the community. He is credited with shaping the elaborate daily service (seva) that still structures worship of Shrinathji, including music, food offerings and seasonal adornment.

Maharana Raj Singh of Mewar was the ruler who received the image in his kingdom in the 1670s.

What happened

According to tradition, the image revealed itself on Govardhan hill near Mathura, and Vallabhacharya recognised it and began its worship there around the end of the fifteenth century. Britannica records that Vallabha "discovered" the image at the foot of Govardhan. The writer Ranjit Hoskote notes that, by tradition, local villagers were already worshipping it before Vallabha came.

The Pushtimarg grew strong in Braj over the next century. According to Hoskote, firmans (imperial orders) show that Mughal emperors from Akbar to Shah Jahan granted the community lands and titles.

This changed under Aurangzeb. In 1669–70 major temples were demolished on his orders, including the Keshava Deva temple at Mathura, in the heart of Krishna's country. The Pushtimarg's custodians decided to take Shrinathji away from Braj. The temple's own account and museum catalogues agree that the move was meant to keep the image safe from Mughal forces. Historians still debate how far Aurangzeb's temple policy was driven by religion and how far by politics. Much of the detail of the journey comes to us through the community's own tradition.

Tradition tells of a long journey: the image was hidden in a bullock cart, stayed some months at Agra, then travelled on through Rajasthan. The temple's account says it took 32 months, because rulers hesitated to take in an image the emperor's forces were pursuing. It was Maharana Raj Singh of Mewar who agreed to give it refuge. In 1672, by the most commonly given date, Shrinathji was installed in a new shrine at Sihad, which grew into Nathdwara.

From temple service to painting. Pushtimarg worship surrounds the deity with beauty: food, flowers, music, jewellery and textiles. The pichwai, literally "that which hangs behind", is the painted cloth set behind the image. The Museum of Art & Photography explains that pichwais were part of matched sets of textiles that also covered walls, canopies, thrones and steps, and that they were changed "daily, seasonally, and for various rituals and festive occasions".

The calendar shows in the cloth. Summer pichwais show lotus ponds and water to suggest coolness. Monsoon ones show dancing peacocks and dark clouds. In winter, painted cloths give way to heavy brocades and embroidery. Festival pichwais show Sharad Purnima, the autumn full moon of the raas dance, or Annakut, the "mountain of food" offered after Krishna lifted Govardhan.

The Metropolitan Museum of Art notes another role: a pichwai could stand in for the deity, so devotees who could not travel to Nathdwara could worship before it.

The paintings were made by families of painters who settled in Nathdwara, mainly from the Adi Gaur, Jangid and Mewara communities. According to the MAP Academy, artists from places such as Kota and Kishangarh joined the Nathdwara workshops, which is why the style is close to Mewar painting. Colours came from minerals, plant and insect sources, and gold and silver. Painters still live and work in the lane called Chitron ki Gali, the street of paintings.

Legends and debates

The chariot that stopped. The best-known story is that at Sihad the wheels of Shrinathji's chariot sank into the mud and would not move. The priests took this as the Lord's choice of home. This is devotional tradition.

Which year? 1672 is the most commonly given date for the installation (the Metropolitan Museum and the temple's own account), but the V&A gives 1671 and other sources give 1670.

How old is pichwai? Very few pichwais carry dates. The MAP Academy notes that the earliest known dated example, from Nathdwara, is from around 1846. Later pichwais show the influence of realism, photography and painters such as Raja Ravi Varma.

Wisdom to carry forward

In Nathdwara, art was not decoration added after worship. It was part of worship itself. Painters, weavers, cooks and musicians all served the same small dark image, and the work changed with the seasons, just as a household does.

The journey also shows that a tradition can survive moving home. What travelled from Braj to Mewar was a whole way of life, and in its new home it grew a new art.

My connection

We visited Shrinathji Temple at Nathdwara on the morning of 18 October, after leaving Kumbhalgarh at about 7:30, and before going on to Eklingji and Udaipur. In my diary I noted that the town is associated with Pichwai paintings, devotional works that depict Shrinathji and seasonal or festival settings, and that this connection between worship and painting naturally interested me. As I wrote then, "In Rajasthan, art so often belongs not only in galleries, but also in the rhythms of devotion."

The next day in Udaipur I met artists who work in the miniature-painting tradition and learned about their processes, including how colours are prepared and how brushes are made. They were not Pichwai painters, but the conversation brought me closer to the kind of patient, inherited skill that this story is about.
