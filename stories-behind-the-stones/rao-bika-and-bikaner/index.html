    A Son’s Kingdom in the Sand — Rao Bika and the Founding of Bikaner

    A father's offhand remark, an uncle's promise and a desert called Jangladesh. How a younger Rathore prince built Bikaner, and why its famous fort came a century later.

    Most cities in Rajasthan are named after the person who founded them, but few founding stories begin with a joke. Bikaner's does, at least as it is usually told: a father teases his son in front of the court, and the son takes the teasing as a dare. Within a generation that son had a capital of his own in the northern Thar.

    The fort visitors queue for today, Junagarh, is not the one that son built. It came about a century later, and keeping those two moments apart is the key to understanding Bikaner.

    Where and when

    Bikaner lies in the north of the Thar Desert, in a dry region that older sources call Jangladesh. Encyclopaedia Britannica dates the start of Rao Bika's conquests in the area to about 1465 and says he began building the city in 1488. The name is usually explained as "the settlement of Bika."

    Bika's family were Rathores, rulers of Marwar to the south-west. His father, Rao Jodha, founded Jodhpur in 1459. Bika did not inherit Jodhpur. Like several of his brothers, he had to win territory for himself.

    Incredible India, the Government of India's tourism site, notes that Bikaner sat on caravan routes and grew into a trading centre. It also says Bika invited merchants to settle there and gave them land.

    The people

    Rao Bika was a son of Rao Jodha, the Rathore ruler who moved his capital from Mandore to Jodhpur. Britannica records Bika's death in 1504.

    Rawat Kandhal was Bika's uncle, Jodha's brother. He went with Bika on the campaign that created the new state.

    Karni Mata was a revered holy woman of the Charan community. Bikaner's rulers came to regard her as their patron goddess, and her temple stands at Deshnok, south of the city.

    Raja Rai Singh ruled Bikaner from 1571. He was one of Emperor Akbar's most distinguished generals and became the first ruler of Bikaner to hold the title raja. His minister Karan Chand oversaw the building of the fort we now call Junagarh.

    What happened

    Bikaner's own tradition places the story's turning point in 1465. Bika came late to his father's court and sat whispering with his uncle Kandhal. Jodha remarked that the two must be plotting to conquer new lands. According to the tradition, uncle and nephew took the remark as a challenge and set out to do exactly that. Accounts say Jodha approved, and that Bika left with only a few hundred men.

    Over the next two decades Bika built up a territory in Jangladesh. He made alliances where he could, including a marriage with the Bhati family of Pugal, and he took advantage of rivalries among the Jat clans who held much of the land. In about 1478 he built a modest stone fort at a site called Rati Ghati. Its remains are said to survive near the Lakshmi Narayan temple in the old city. Britannica gives 1488 as the year he began building the city, and that is the date Bikaner usually celebrates as its founding.

    When Bika died in 1504, Bikaner was a real but small state. The decisive change came with Rai Singh, who reigned from 1571 until 1611 or 1612 (sources differ). His service as a Mughal general brought the wealth that paid for a far grander fortress. Construction of its walls and moat is generally dated from 1589 to 1594, under the supervision of his minister Karan Chand. The fort was first called Chintamani. It became known as Junagarh, the "old fort", only in the early twentieth century, after the royal family moved to the new Lalgarh Palace.

    Later rulers added palaces inside it, such as Anup Mahal, Chandra Mahal and the twentieth-century Ganga Mahal. The painted and mirrored interiors built up over many reigns. None of them date from Bika's lifetime.

    Legends and debates

    The founding remark. The story of Jodha's teasing comes from Rathore and Bikaner tradition. It is best read as a story the dynasty told about itself, not a verbatim record.

    Karni Mata's role. According to devotional tradition, Karni Mata blessed Bika, predicted that his fame would surpass his father's, and laid the foundation of his fort at Rati Ghati in 1485. These details show how closely Bikaner's rulers tied their legitimacy to her, but they are matters of faith, not documented history.

    The name. James Tod, the nineteenth-century British writer, recorded a story that the land belonged to a Jat called Naira (or Nera), who gave it up on condition that his name be joined to Bika's: Bika + Ner = Bikaner. It is a tradition Tod recorded, not a settled etymology.

    Dates. Sources variously give 1465, 1472, 1478, 1485 and 1488. The safest summary: Bika began carving out his territory from about 1465, and the city is conventionally dated to 1488.

    "Never conquered." Junagarh is often called a fort that was never taken. Yet the government tourism page for the fort itself mentions that the Mughal prince Kamran Mirza briefly occupied Bikaner's fort in 1534, before Rai Singh's rebuilding. "Rarely taken, and never held for long" is more accurate.

    Wisdom to carry forward

    The building Bikaner is famous for was not made by its founder. Bika's own fort is now mostly gone. What he left behind was harder to photograph: a territory, alliances, settled merchants and a dynasty. Without that groundwork, Rai Singh's wealth would have had nothing to build on.

    In front of a magnificent facade, it is worth asking two questions: who paid for this, and who made it possible to build here at all? The answers are often different people, a century apart.

    My connection

    We reached Bikaner around 2 p.m. on the fourth day of our journey, after a morning in Pushkar. At Junagarh, what struck me first was that it stands on relatively level ground, not high on a hill like so many Rajasthani forts. We visited the fort and the Prachina Gallery, and I loved the details: floral patterns, painted surfaces, decorative rooms and doors with Radha and Krishna imagery. There was so much craftsmanship in one place that I hardly knew where to point the camera next. As I wrote in my diary, "for me the deeper fascination lies in the hands that made them: painters, woodcarvers, stoneworkers, mirror specialists and generations of artisans whose work outlived them." Learning that these rooms were added over centuries, long after Rao Bika, makes that thought even truer.

    Afterwards, at the palace café, we met Rajkumari Siddhi Kumari Ji of Bikaner's former royal family. She agreed warmly to a photograph. It felt like a small, living link to the history inside those walls.
