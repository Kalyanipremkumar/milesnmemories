Day 9 — Nathdwara, Eklingji and Udaipur

Day 9, Sunday 18 October

Towards Udaipur: temples, gardens and an evening of culture

A day that began with devotion and ended in the old city.

In this chapter

  1. Shrinathji and Eklingji
  2. First impressions of Udaipur
  3. A full evening in the old city

We left Kumbhalgarh at about 7:30 in the morning and travelled towards Nathdwara. The first part of our day was devoted to two important temples, each deeply connected with Rajasthan’s religious and royal history.

Shrinathji and Eklingji

At Nathdwara we visited Shrinathji Temple, dedicated to a form of Krishna especially revered in the Pushtimarg tradition. The town is also associated with Pichwai paintings, devotional works that depict Shrinathji and seasonal or festival settings. That connection between worship and painting naturally interested me. In Rajasthan, art so often belongs not only in galleries, but also in the rhythms of devotion.

We then visited Eklingji Temple, associated with Lord Shiva and the historic rulers of Mewar. Eklingji’s significance goes beyond its architecture: the deity has traditionally been regarded as the spiritual sovereign of Mewar, with its rulers serving in a custodial role. After years of visiting sacred sites across India, it was meaningful to include these temples in our Rajasthan itinerary.

First impressions of Udaipur

We reached Udaipur around 2 p.m., had lunch and checked into Hotel Udaigarh in the old-city area near Lake Pichola. After so many days on the road, the city immediately felt different to me. Compared with many stretches we had travelled through, I found Udaipur cleaner and neater, with wider roads in the areas we saw. It would eventually become my favourite city of the trip.

We visited Saheliyon Ki Bari, the garden associated with the women of the Mewar royal household, known for its fountains, water features and landscaped spaces. We also spent time at Fateh Sagar Lake, one of the city’s best-known waterside places. The contrast with the desert landscapes we had recently left behind was striking.

A full evening in the old city

From six in the evening we continued exploring. Gangaur Ghat offered views across Lake Pichola and a sense of the old city at the water’s edge. We visited Bagore Ki Haveli and attended the Dharohar folk-dance programme, an enjoyable introduction to performance traditions beyond the music we had heard at the desert camp. We also visited Jagdish Temple, explored shops and markets, and enjoyed the lively atmosphere of the lanes around the lake.

This was a full day: temples in the morning, gardens and lakes in the afternoon, then music, dance, architecture and shopping in the evening. We had already experienced many sides of Rajasthan, but Udaipur seemed to gather several of them into one place.
