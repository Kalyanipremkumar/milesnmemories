Day 1, Saturday 10 October

Jaipur: first impressions of the Pink City

An ordinary airport, an extraordinary old city, and an artistic welcome.

We began while Bengaluru was still asleep. Our flight left at 1:10 in the morning, and after a connection through Ahmedabad we reached Jaipur on the early morning flight. I had waited more than a decade to come to Rajasthan, so perhaps my expectations were unusually high. Even the airport, I imagined, might greet visitors with a hint of the state’s royal heritage. Instead it felt rather ordinary — and the heat was immediate. It was not quite the grand welcome I had pictured!

We stayed at Arya Niwas, conveniently placed for exploring the old city. That choice worked beautifully: it was an economical, comfortable base, and we could reach the main sights without making every outing a major expedition. What caught my attention first, though, was not the convenience. It was the Mithila artwork on the walls. I practise and admire traditional Indian art, so seeing familiar motifs in a Jaipur hotel was an unexpected pleasure. One long painting, around five metres wide, was especially striking. Breakfast had another memorable detail: live sitar music. It was a lovely way to begin a day of sightseeing.

Why “pink” when I see terracotta?

After freshening up, we entered the old walled city. Suddenly the Jaipur I had imagined began to appear. Buildings stretched along the streets in a coordinated terracotta shade. Shop signs followed a similar visual pattern. Amid the bustle of traffic and commerce, there was an astonishing sense of uniformity. I kept looking from one building to the next, wondering how a living, busy city had retained such a distinct visual identity.

I asked our guide a question that had been bothering me: why call it the Pink City when the colour looked terracotta? Jaipur’s buildings were famously painted for the 1876 visit of the Prince of Wales, later King Edward VII, with pink associated with hospitality. Our guide added an anecdote: that the visiting prince described the terracotta shade as pink and the description stayed. I enjoyed that explanation, although I treat that particular detail as the guide’s telling rather than a settled historical fact. Whatever the precise origin of the nickname, the city’s colour was unforgettable.

Four landmarks, four different stories

At City Palace we stepped into the history of Jaipur’s rulers and their taste for courtyards, ornament and ceremonial spaces. Jaipur itself was founded in 1727 by Sawai Jai Singh II, and the city’s planned layout is part of what makes it remarkable. Its historic walled city is recognised by UNESCO.

Jantar Mantar offered a very different kind of wonder. Here, enormous instruments of stone and masonry were built to observe the heavens and measure time. The site is a reminder that the ruler who founded Jaipur was deeply interested in astronomy. At Hawa Mahal, the honeycomb façade and rows of small windows showed another side of architecture: a building that is instantly recognisable, yet designed around climate, privacy and courtly life. Jal Mahal, appearing to float in Man Sagar Lake, gave us a quieter scene to look at.

I liked all four places. Rather than selecting one as the winner, I enjoyed how different they were. By evening we had also seen the shops and markets of the Pink City. What stayed with me was not just the list of monuments, but the colour of the streets, the consistency of the signboards, the artwork at our hotel and the music over breakfast. Rajasthan was beginning to feel like a place where history and art could appear at any moment, even between the major attractions.
