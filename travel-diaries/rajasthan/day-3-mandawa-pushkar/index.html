    Day 3 — Mandawa and Pushkar

    Day 3, Monday 12 October

    Mandawa: painted walls and a dream haveli

    Six havelis, countless frescoes, and one entirely reasonable request to Prem.

    In this chapter

    1. Why Shekhawati is a painted world
    2. The haveli I wanted to take home
    3. A late arrival in Pushkar

    We left Jaipur and travelled towards Mandawa in the Shekhawati region. If Jaipur had impressed me with its planned streets and royal architecture, Mandawa was about the art of the home — and about the extraordinary ambitions of merchant families who commissioned buildings that still astonish visitors.

    Why Shekhawati is a painted world

    The towns of Shekhawati became prosperous through trade and merchant networks. Wealthy families built grand havelis, often arranged around courtyards, with elaborately decorated entrances, rooms and façades. Their walls became painted surfaces. Frescoes and murals depict deities, mythological episodes, everyday scenes, flowers, animals and, in some places, images inspired by changing fashions and modern technology. Walking through them feels rather like turning the pages of a vast illustrated book.

    We visited six havelis. Everywhere I looked there was something to photograph: painted ceilings, carved wooden doors, elephant motifs, delicate borders, courtyards, gateways and fading yet beautiful scenes on plaster. Some paintings had weathered with time; others still carried strong colour. The combination of art and architecture was extraordinary. I found myself wondering what everyday life must have been like inside these homes, when their courtyards were full of people and their walls newly painted.

    The haveli I wanted to take home

    At some point, surrounded by all that beauty, I asked Prem to buy me a haveli. It was said with a smile, of course — but only partly as a joke! I could easily imagine spending even one day in a home like that, surrounded by frescoes, old wooden doors and courtyards filled with stories. Prem has patiently photographed so many things that catch my eye; this time, I was mentally planning an entire future haveli.

    One particularly happy surprise was seeing framed Mithila or Madhubani-style artwork in a haveli. As someone who loves that tradition, I noticed it immediately. My eye kept returning to the familiar rhythms of lines, birds, trees and decorative patterns. Rajasthan’s painted walls were offering me new ideas, but also making me feel connected to the art I already knew.

    For readers who visit Mandawa, I would recommend taking time to look beyond the largest murals. Study the edges of doorways, the smaller motifs around windows and the way the painting relates to the architecture. That is where some of the loveliest details live. These are historic homes and fragile artworks, so respectful visiting matters.

    A late arrival in Pushkar

    By night we reached Pushkar, around 9:30 p.m., and checked into Athithi Resort. After the visual abundance of Mandawa, the property felt calm and green. There were cottage-style rooms, a veranda and amla trees carrying plenty of fruit. Traditional Rajasthani wall scenes added to the atmosphere. The following morning, with breakfast and a little time to notice the surroundings, the resort became more than just an overnight stop. It was another reminder that sometimes a hotel is memorable for one small detail — in this case, those amla trees.
