Day 4 — Pushkar and Bikaner

Day 4, Tuesday 13 October

Pushkar to Bikaner: temples, camels and a princess

A sacred lake, a royal fort and an encounter we never planned.

In this chapter

  1. Camels and the desert economy
  2. Junagarh: the palace of details
  3. Coffee, a conversation and an unexpected photograph
  4. The Rampuria Havelis

Pushkar has a very different atmosphere from the fortified cities we had been exploring. Its identity is closely tied to pilgrimage, to the sacred lake and to the rare, well-known Brahma Temple. We visited the temple and the ghats around Pushkar Lake, where the steps and water form the centre of the town’s spiritual life. We also went to Savitri Mata Temple using the ropeway, which gave us another perspective on the surrounding landscape.

We continued towards Bikaner, stopping for lunch on the way, and arrived around 2 p.m. Our day there combined two worlds that might seem unrelated at first: the desert’s relationship with camels and the lavish decorative traditions of a royal court.

Camels and the desert economy

At the National Research Centre on Camel, we encountered an animal that has been central to life and transport in arid Rajasthan. Camels are often presented to tourists as picturesque silhouettes against dunes, but their importance runs much deeper: mobility, livelihoods, milk, adaptation and research. It was interesting to see that side of the region before moving on to the city’s royal monuments.

Junagarh: the palace of details

Junagarh Fort is especially notable because, unlike many famous Rajasthani forts, it stands on relatively level ground rather than high on a hill. Inside are richly decorated palace spaces, painted ceilings, carved doors, mirrors and elaborate ornament. We visited the fort and the Prachina Gallery. I loved the details: floral patterns, painted surfaces, decorative rooms, and doors with Radha and Krishna imagery. I kept finding new things to look at. There was so much craftsmanship concentrated in one place that it was difficult to know where to point the camera next.

It is tempting to describe palaces only in terms of kings, battles and dates. But for me the deeper fascination lies in the hands that made them: painters, woodcarvers, stoneworkers, mirror specialists and generations of artisans whose work outlived them. Junagarh made that thought especially vivid.

Coffee, a conversation and an unexpected photograph

After exploring the palace and gallery, we were tired. We stopped at the palace café for water and coffee. A woman was seated nearby, speaking with someone. I overheard the word “princess” and became curious. When I asked the café staff, I learned that she was Rajkumari Siddhi Kumari Ji, a member of Bikaner’s former royal family and a public representative who had been elected to the Rajasthan Legislative Assembly.

I approached her, greeted her and asked whether we could take a photograph. I also asked if I could share it on social media. Her response was warm and simple: “Absolutely!” What had started as a short break for coffee turned into one of the unexpected memories of our journey. I was glad I had asked rather than simply wondering who she was after we left.

The Rampuria Havelis

In Bikaner’s old city we also walked past the Rampuria Havelis, the mansions built by the Rampuria merchant family. Their red sandstone facades are covered in carved jharokhas, brackets and screens, worked so finely that the stone looks almost like wood. Standing in those narrow lanes, it was easy to see how much of Bikaner’s wealth once came from its traders, and how much of it they spent on beauty.

We stayed that night at Harasar Haveli. By then, Rajasthan had already given us temples, painted merchant homes, grand forts and a spontaneous meeting with someone connected to its living royal history. The trip was becoming wonderfully varied.
