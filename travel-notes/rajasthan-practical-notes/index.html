    Rajasthan: practical notes from our eleven days

    A few practical details from our experience. Not a sponsored recommendation.

    Route and timing

    We travelled from 10 to 20 October 2026, moving through Jaipur, Mandawa, Pushkar, Bikaner, Jaisalmer, Khuri, Jodhpur, Ranakpur, Kumbhalgarh, Nathdwara and Udaipur. It was an ambitious itinerary. If you prefer long, unhurried museum visits, you may want to spend more nights in a few places rather than covering the same distance.

    Costs and transport

    Our total trip expenditure was approximately ₹1.3 lakh, excluding shopping. For the intercity portion, we paid ₹37,000 for a cab from Jaipur, beginning on our third day, through the drop in Udaipur. This included tolls and driver-related charges; local Jaipur transport was separate. A Rajasthani gentleman in his mid-sixties, who worked as a French-speaking guide, helped arrange the cab. He was direct and straightforward. Our driver was not very flexible at first, but the experience improved as we went along. These are our 2026 costs and should not be treated as current quotations.

    Places we stayed

    Arya Niwas in Jaipur was economical and convenient, with Mithila artwork and breakfast-time sitar music that I still remember. Athithi Resort in Pushkar had cottages and lovely amla trees. Harasar Haveli was our Bikaner stay. Heritage Inn in Jaisalmer was not a favourite. Registhan Desert Camp at Khuri gave us a memorable night under the stars despite disappointing food. Rohet Garh in Rohet village was my favourite hotel experience: clean, comfortable and full of royal atmosphere. We also stayed at Kumbhalgarh Junagarh Resort, then Udaigarh and Panna Vilas in Udaipur.

    What I would tell another art lover

    Make time for Mandawa’s frescoes; do not rush through its havelis. Look closely at carved doors, ceiling borders and the smaller details in palace rooms. In Udaipur, try to meet working artists and ask about their techniques, respectfully and without assuming that every studio visit will be available. Shilpgram can be a rewarding place to purchase directly from artisans. If you collect inspiration for your own work, take notes about where you photographed each motif.

    A few honest reminders

    The weather can be hot even when the calendar suggests autumn. Build in rest between long drives and sightseeing. Hotel quality varies; confirm current reviews and accessibility, especially in narrow old-city lanes. The desert camp is as much about the setting and night sky as about comfort. Be considerate at temples, active residential havelis and places of worship, and check photography rules before taking pictures.

    This is a personal account, not a sponsored guide. Experiences and prices refer to October 2026; verify access, hours and costs before travelling.

    A state of many colours and stories

    The cities we visited, and the words that appear throughout the diary.

    Jaipur The Pink City
    Founded in 1727 by Sawai Jai Singh II, Jaipur is renowned for its planned streets and orderly bazaars. In 1876, Maharaja Sawai Ram Singh II had the city painted pink to welcome the Prince of Wales. The historic buildings often look more terracotta or salmon-coloured than bright pink. The walled city was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2019.
    Jodhpur The Blue City
    Founded in 1459 by Rao Jodha, Jodhpur became the centre of Marwar. Blue-washed houses, especially in older quarters below Mehrangarh Fort, gave it its nickname. The colour has traditionally been linked to Brahmin households; explanations involving cooling or deterring insects are also commonly repeated, though they are not definitive. Jodhpur is also called the Sun City.
    Jaisalmer The Golden City
    Founded in 1156 by Rawal Jaisal, Jaisalmer owes its warm golden appearance to local yellow sandstone. Unlike Jaipur, the colour comes mainly from the building material rather than a citywide painting order.
    Udaipur The City of Lakes, the White City
    Maharana Udai Singh II founded Udaipur in 1553 as a new capital of Mewar. Lake Pichola, Fateh Sagar and other lakes shaped its water supply, landscape and lakeside architecture. For me, Udaipur was also the city of miniature-painting artists, lakeside views and a noticeably cleaner urban experience.
    Bikaner The Camel City
    Founded by Rao Bika in the late fifteenth century, Bikaner grew in a region where camels were essential to desert transport and livelihoods. It is also celebrated for Junagarh Fort, painted palace rooms, merchant havelis and distinctive food traditions. “Camel City” is a popular nickname, not an official historical title.
    Shekhawati and Mandawa An open-air art gallery
    Shekhawati’s merchant families commissioned elaborately painted havelis, especially during the eighteenth to early twentieth centuries. Their frescoes depict deities, courtly scenes, flora, animals and, later, trains and other modern subjects. Walking through its painted courtyards, I could easily imagine a haveli of my own.
    Pushkar The sacred town of Brahma
    Pushkar is associated with Brahma, its celebrated temple and the sacred Pushkar Lake. In local religious tradition, the lake arose where Brahma’s lotus fell. Pushkar is also known for its annual livestock fair and rose cultivation. The lotus-origin story belongs to religious tradition, not archaeological history.

    Words you will meet in the diary

    Haveli
    A traditional mansion, often built around courtyards and decorated with paintings or carved details.
    Jharokha
    A projecting enclosed balcony or window, often elaborately carved.
    Chhatri
    A domed pavilion, often used above memorials or on palaces and rooftops.
    Baori, baoli, kund
    A stepwell with stairs descending toward water; kund often refers to a stepped water tank.
    Pol
    A gateway, particularly to a fort, palace or walled precinct.
    Zenana
    The residential quarters traditionally reserved for women in a palace or large household.
    Sheesh Mahal
    Literally “palace of mirrors”; a room or hall decorated with mirrorwork.
    Fresco
    A wall-painting technique; also used broadly for Shekhawati’s painted walls.
    Pichwai
    A devotional textile painting, particularly associated with Shrinathji and Nathdwara.
    Miniature painting
    A highly detailed tradition often associated with royal ateliers; it describes fine-scale technique, not always a tiny picture.
    Kachni and bharni
    Line-based and filled-colour approaches in Mithila painting, an art form close to my heart.
    Ghat
    Steps leading down to a river or lake, used for worship, bathing or gathering.
