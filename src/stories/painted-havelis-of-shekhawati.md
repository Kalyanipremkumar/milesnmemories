In a Mandawa courtyard, you can look up and see Krishna on one wall, a row of elephants with their mahouts on another, and, tucked into a border, a steam train puffing across the plaster. The people who paid for these paintings often spent most of their lives hundreds of kilometres away, in the counting houses of Calcutta and Bombay.

That is the puzzle at the heart of Shekhawati. In a semi-desert region of small towns in northern Rajasthan, there are hundreds of painted mansions, temples, wells and memorials. Many of them were built by families who had already moved away. Why decorate a home you hardly live in?

## Where and when

Shekhawati covers parts of today's Jhunjhunu, Sikar and Churu districts, an arid region north-west of Jaipur. It takes its name from **Rao Shekha**, a Kachwaha Rajput chief (a branch of the same clan that ruled Amer) who established his authority there in the mid-fifteenth century. By the 1730s, his descendants, the Shekhawat *thakurs*, had divided the region into semi-independent estates, each with fortified towns and carefully managed water sources such as stepwells and tanks.

UNESCO's tentative-list entry, submitted by India in June 2026, explains what happened next. Between about 1740 and 1800, towns on the caravan routes grew quickly, because the thakurs offered merchants security and tax incentives to settle. **Mandawa** is one example. Its fort was built by Thakur Nawal Singh, and Outlook Traveller dates it to 1755. Trading families settled in the town around it.

## The people

The merchants were Hindu and Jain trading families. Outside Rajasthan they came to be called **Marwaris**, a broad label that, as the historian Abhishek Parashar notes, often covers people with no connection to Marwar (Jodhpur) itself. In fact, he writes, most of the leading Marwari entrepreneurs came from Shekhawati.

Some of India's best-known business families trace their roots here. The Birlas, for example, came from Pilani in Jhunjhunu district. In Mandawa today, havelis carry the names of families such as the Goenkas, Morarkas and Jhunjhunwalas.

The painters, local craftsmen, are mostly unnamed; the havelis carry their patrons' names.

## What happened

In the nineteenth century, overland caravan trade declined as commerce shifted to the British-run ports. Many Shekhawati merchants followed the money to Calcutta, Bombay and other colonial centres. They worked as traders, brokers, bankers and insurers, dealing in cotton, jute and opium. A drought-prone homeland pushed them; railways and credit networks made distant business possible.

They did not cut their ties with home. From the mid-nineteenth century until about the 1930s, they sent money back to build in their home towns. UNESCO dates the most intense period of patronage to the 1830s through the early twentieth century, with haveli painting at its peak in the middle and later 1800s. A family that wanted to be recognised as an established *seth* was expected to build more than a house: a temple, a well, a rest house for travellers and, after a patriarch's death, a domed *chhatri* in his memory.

The havelis were usually arranged around courtyards. The outer one was for business and male visitors, and the inner one for family life. Their walls were painted in *fresco buono* (pigment on wet lime plaster) and *fresco secco* (on dry plaster), using mineral and plant colours and, later, imported synthetic pigments.

The subjects changed with the times. Earlier paintings are full of gods and goddesses, scenes from the epics, folk tales such as the ballad of Dhola and Maru, and processions of elephants and horses. Later ones show British officers, trains, motorcars, gramophones and hot-air balloons. These were new things the merchants had seen in the cities and wanted on their own walls.

Some families commissioned havelis late in the nineteenth century after every member had moved away, so a few grand houses were built but barely lived in. As the community settled permanently in the cities during the twentieth century, the painting tradition faded. By the middle of that century it had largely stopped.

## Legends and debates

**Why paint a house you have left?** The usual explanation is display, and that is part of it. But writers drawing on scholars such as Anne Hardgrove and Kaiwan Mehta suggest something more. The painted buildings helped make the town a "homeland", a place where a family's standing was publicly recorded, in contrast to the cities where it actually did business. The havelis were as much about belonging as about showing off.

**Dates and numbers.** Figures vary. One magazine estimates about 2,000 frescoed buildings, painted from the seventeenth to the early twentieth century. UNESCO's entry focuses on 14 of roughly 38 historic towns. Sources also disagree on when building started (anything from the mid-eighteenth century to the 1840s), depending on whether they mean forts and early temples or the great wave of merchant havelis.

**Satire or simply news?** The stiff, overdressed Europeans in some later paintings are sometimes read as gentle mockery of the colonial rulers. They may just as well show painters recording something new and strange. There is no written evidence either way.

**Survival.** Nearly all the havelis are privately owned. Some are decaying; a few have been restored as museums or heritage hotels.

## Wisdom to carry forward

These families made their fortunes far away, yet they spent much of it on walls in towns they rarely saw. The paintings were a way of saying "we still belong here".

When you see trains on the walls next to Krishna, you are looking at people who brought the new world home and set it beside the old one, without throwing either away.

## My connection

From Jaipur we travelled to Mandawa. If Jaipur had impressed me with its planned streets and royal architecture, Mandawa "was about the art of the home — and about the extraordinary ambitions of merchant families who commissioned buildings that still astonish visitors." We visited six havelis. Everywhere I looked there was something to photograph: painted ceilings, carved wooden doors, elephant motifs, delicate borders and fading yet beautiful scenes on plaster. I kept wondering what everyday life was like when those courtyards were full of people and the walls were newly painted.

Surrounded by all that beauty, I asked Prem to buy me a haveli. I said it with a smile, but only partly as a joke! In one haveli I was delighted to find framed Mithila or Madhubani-style artwork, and my eye kept returning to its familiar lines, birds and trees. Later, in Bikaner and Jaisalmer, I saw other merchant havelis carved in stone rather than painted. Looking back at the end of the trip, I wrote that Mandawa's frescoes may have given me some of my richest artistic inspiration.
