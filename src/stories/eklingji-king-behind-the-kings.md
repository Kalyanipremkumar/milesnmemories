What if a kingdom believed that its true ruler was not the Maharana sitting on the throne, but Lord Shiva himself?

That is the fascinating tradition behind Eklingji Temple, located in Kailashpuri, near Udaipur.

## Where and when

Kailashpuri lies on the road between Nathdwara and Udaipur, in the hills of the old Mewar heartland. It is close to Nagda, one of the earliest seats of Mewar's Guhila rulers. Eklingji is the family deity of the Mewar royal house. Lonely Planet describes it as "an incarnation of Shiva", and the shrine has been at the centre of the kingdom's religious life for many centuries. ⟨added⟩

Much of what we know about the temple's early story comes from the *Ekalinga Mahatmya*, a Sanskrit text composed in the fifteenth century under the patronage of Rana Kumbha. It records much older traditions, so its account of the eighth century was written down some seven hundred years after the events it describes. ⟨added⟩

The temple itself has witnessed centuries of history. Its present form reflects later rebuilding and restoration, including work associated with Rana Raimal. At the heart of the shrine is a four-faced image of Shiva carved from black stone.

Each face is traditionally associated with a different aspect of the divine.

## The people

The temple is traditionally associated with Bappa Rawal, an eighth-century ruler of Mewar. According to the royal tradition, Bappa Rawal received the blessings of the sage Harit Rashi, who taught him to regard Lord Eklingji as the supreme ruler of Mewar.

Later chronicles tell how Bappa grew up in hiding near Nagda, tending cattle, before Harit Rashi initiated him into the worship of Shiva and told him to build the temple. The *Ekalinga Mahatmya* dates his rule to the early eighth century (it gives 728 CE for his founding of Mewar). The House of Mewar itself counts its custodianship from 734 CE. ⟨added⟩

Many later rulers left their mark on the temple. Hammir Singh, who re-established the family's rule in the fourteenth century, carried out major renovations. Rana Mokal (r. 1421–1433) renovated it again. Rana Kumbha (r. 1433–1468) rebuilt it. Rana Raimal (r. 1473–1509) undertook the last major rebuilding of the complex and installed the present image. ⟨added⟩

## What happened

From then on, the Maharanas of Mewar considered themselves not the owners of the kingdom, but its Diwans — representatives entrusted with governing on behalf of Lord Shiva.

It is an extraordinary idea. A powerful ruler could command armies, build forts and administer a kingdom, yet acknowledge a higher authority.

The connection between Eklingji and the rulers of Mewar was not merely ceremonial. It became part of the dynasty's understanding of kingship, duty and legitimacy.

Inscriptions show how seriously this was taken. In an inscription of 1460, Rana Kumbha describes himself as the personal servant of Eklingji. The rulers' shared title, *Ekling-ka-Diwan*, expressed the same idea: the god was sovereign and the ruler was his minister. ⟨added⟩

The temple also suffered in Mewar's wars. Live History India notes that it was damaged by invading armies and rebuilt several times. After an attack in the late fifteenth century by Ghiyath Shah, the sultan of Malwa, Rana Raimal is said to have used the ransom from Ghiyath Shah's capture to pay for rebuilding it. What visitors see today largely dates from that fifteenth-century work. Lonely Planet describes the image at its heart as "a towering four-faced, black-marble image of Eklingji". ⟨added⟩

The tradition is still alive. Lakshyaraj Singh Mewar, a member of the former royal family, told ThePrint that Mewar's rulers "have always been its sewak (caretakers), not its kings", and that the real crown rests with Eklingnath ji. ⟨added⟩

## Legends and debates

**Bappa Rawal's identity.** Bappa Rawal is a figure of tradition as much as of history. His name is not in the earliest Guhila inscription (Atpur, 977 CE), and later records list him inconsistently. Some historians think "Bappa Rawal" was a title or nickname, possibly for a ruler known from inscriptions as Kalabhoja. The meeting with Harit Rashi belongs to the dynasty's sacred tradition, not to documented history. ⟨added⟩

**The image.** Descriptions vary. Some call the image four-faced, while others describe five faces, four looking outwards with a lingam rising between them. Live History India gives the faces as Brahma (west), Vishnu (north), Surya (east) and Rudra, a form of Shiva (south). ⟨added⟩

**The title "Maharana".** Bappa's own title was *Rawal*. The title *Rana* belonged to the Sisodia branch that took power in the fourteenth century, and *Maharana* is later still. The idea of ruling as Eklingji's minister is very old, but the word "Maharana" fits the later centuries better than Bappa's time. ⟨added⟩

## Wisdom to carry forward

Power is not the same as ownership.

The tradition of Eklingji offers a thought worth carrying beyond the temple: leadership can be understood as a responsibility held in trust, rather than a privilege possessed forever.

Standing before a temple so closely connected with Mewar's history, one is reminded that even kings believed they were answerable to something greater than themselves.
