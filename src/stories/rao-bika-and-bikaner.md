Most cities in Rajasthan are named after the person who founded them, but few founding stories begin with a joke. Bikaner's does, at least as it is usually told: a father teases his son in front of the court, and the son takes the teasing as a dare. Within a generation that son had a capital of his own in the northern Thar.

The fort visitors queue for today, Junagarh, is not the fort that son built. It belongs to a great-great-grandson's era, roughly a hundred years later. Keeping those two moments apart is the key to understanding Bikaner: a determined founder on one side, and a wealthy, Mughal-connected court on the other.

## Where and when

Bikaner lies in the north of the Thar Desert, in a dry region that older sources call Jangladesh. Encyclopaedia Britannica dates the start of Rao Bika's conquests in the area to about 1465 and says he began building the city in 1488. The name is usually explained as "the settlement of Bika."

This was the late fifteenth century, when Rajput clans across Rajasthan were competing for land and water. Bika's family were Rathores, rulers of Marwar to the south-west. His father, Rao Jodha, founded Jodhpur in 1459. Bika did not inherit Jodhpur. Like several of his brothers, he had to win territory for himself.

The location mattered. Incredible India, the Government of India's tourism site, notes that Bikaner sat on caravan routes and grew into a trading centre. Bika is said to have invited merchants to settle there and given them land. Generations later, merchant families built the carved havelis of the old city.

## The people

**Rao Bika** was a son of Rao Jodha of Marwar. Britannica records his death in 1504.

**Rao Jodha**, his father, was the Rathore ruler who moved his capital from Mandore and founded Jodhpur and Mehrangarh.

**Rawat Kandhal** was Bika's uncle, Jodha's brother. He went with Bika on the campaign that created the new state.

**Karni Mata** was a revered holy woman of the Charan community. Bikaner's rulers came to regard her as their patron goddess, and her temple stands at Deshnok, south of the city.

**Raja Rai Singh** ruled Bikaner from 1571. He was one of Emperor Akbar's most distinguished generals and became the first ruler of Bikaner to hold the title *raja*. His minister **Karan Chand** oversaw the building of the fort we now call Junagarh.

## What happened

Bikaner's own tradition places the story's turning point in 1465. Bika came late to his father's court and sat whispering with his uncle Kandhal. Jodha remarked that the two must be plotting to conquer new lands. According to the tradition, uncle and nephew took the remark as a challenge and set out to do exactly that. Accounts say Jodha approved of the venture. The number of men who went with them varies: one figure is 100 horsemen and 500 foot soldiers, another is a few hundred clansmen.

Over the next two decades Bika built up a territory in Jangladesh. He made alliances where he could, including a marriage with the Bhati family of Pugal, and he took advantage of rivalries among the Jat clans who held much of the land. In about 1478 he built a modest stone fort at a site called Rati Ghati. Its remains are said to survive near the Lakshmi Narayan temple in the old city. Britannica gives 1488 as the year he began building the city, and that is the date Bikaner usually celebrates as its founding.

When Bika died in 1504, Bikaner was a real state, but a small one. It grew under his successors. The decisive change came with Rai Singh, who reigned from 1571 until 1611 or 1612 (sources differ on the final year). He served the Mughals as a general, and the wealth and prestige that came with it paid for a new and far grander fortress. Construction of its walls and moat is generally dated from 1589 to 1594, under the supervision of his minister Karan Chand. The fort was first called **Chintamani**. It became known as Junagarh, the "old fort", only in the early twentieth century, after the royal family moved to the new Lalgarh Palace.

Over the next three centuries later rulers added palaces inside it: Karan Mahal, Anup Mahal, Chandra Mahal, Phool Mahal and the twentieth-century Ganga Mahal. The painted and mirrored interiors visitors see today built up gradually over many reigns. None of them were made in Bika's lifetime.

## Legends and debates

**The founding remark.** The story of Jodha's teasing comes from Rathore and Bikaner tradition. It is widely repeated, and it fits a family in which several sons founded their own towns. It should still be read as a story the dynasty told about itself, not a verbatim record.

**Karni Mata's role.** According to devotional tradition, Karni Mata blessed Bika, predicted that his fame would surpass his father's, advised him where not to build, and laid the foundation of his fort at Rati Ghati in 1485. These details come mainly from a devotional biography of the goddess. They show how closely Bikaner's rulers tied their legitimacy to her, but they are matters of faith, not documented history.

**The name.** James Tod, the nineteenth-century British writer, recorded a story that the land belonged to a Jat called Naira (or Nera), who gave it up on condition that his name be joined to Bika's: Bika + Ner = Bikaner. Most modern references simply say "settlement of Bika." Treat the Naira story as a tradition Tod recorded, not a settled etymology.

**Dates.** Different sources give 1465 (Bika's departure), 1472 (an early reign or founding date in some lists), 1478 or 1485 (the first fort) and 1488 (the city). The safest summary: Bika began carving out his territory from about 1465, and the city is conventionally dated to 1488.

**"Never conquered."** Junagarh is often called a fort that was never taken. Several accounts, including the government tourism page for the fort, mention that the Mughal prince Kamran Mirza briefly occupied it in 1534. The claim needs a qualifier: rarely taken, and never held for long. Note also that 1534 is before Rai Singh's rebuilding, so the fort Kamran entered was the earlier one.

## Wisdom to carry forward

The building Bikaner is famous for was not made by its founder. Bika's own fort was plain enough to be replaced and is now mostly gone. What he left behind was harder to photograph: a territory, alliances, a settled merchant community, and the start of a line of rulers. Without that groundwork there would have been nothing for Rai Singh's wealth to build on.

So when I stand in front of a magnificent facade, I try to ask two questions. Who paid for it? And who made it possible for anyone to build here at all? The answers are often different people, a century apart.

## My connection

We reached Bikaner around 2 p.m. on the fourth day of our journey, after a morning in Pushkar. Before the royal monuments we visited the National Research Centre on Camel, a useful reminder that camels have been central to life and transport in this region.

At Junagarh, what struck me first was that it stands on relatively level ground, not high on a hill like so many Rajasthani forts. We visited the fort and the Prachina Gallery, and I loved the details: floral patterns, painted surfaces, decorative rooms and doors with Radha and Krishna imagery. There was so much craftsmanship in one place that I hardly knew where to point the camera next. As I wrote in my diary, "for me the deeper fascination lies in the hands that made them: painters, woodcarvers, stoneworkers, mirror specialists and generations of artisans whose work outlived them." Learning that these rooms were added over centuries, long after Rao Bika, makes that thought even truer.

Afterwards, at the palace café, we met Rajkumari Siddhi Kumari Ji of Bikaner's former royal family. She agreed warmly to a photograph. It felt like a small, living link to the history inside those walls. In the old city we also walked past the Rampuria Havelis, where it was easy to see how much of Bikaner's wealth came from its traders.
