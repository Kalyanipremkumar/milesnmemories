Udaipur feels like a city that was always meant to be there, with its palaces rising from the edge of Lake Pichola and white buildings folded into the hills. In fact it was a new capital, created by a ruler of Mewar who was looking for somewhere safer than his ancestral fortress of Chittorgarh.

That ruler was Maharana Udai Singh II, and history has not always been kind to him. His life began with one of Rajasthan's most famous rescue stories, and it ended in exile in the hills. In between, he made the decision that gave Mewar its future capital.

## Where and when

In the early sixteenth century, Mewar's capital was the great hill fort of Chittorgarh. Udai Singh was born there on 4 August 1522 (the date given in standard accounts). He was a son of Rana Sanga, the famous Rajput king, and Rani Karnavati. After Sanga's death the kingdom went through years of turmoil: wars with Gujarat, a sack of Chittor in 1535, and murders within the royal family.

The site he later chose for his new capital lay in the Girwa valley to the south-west. This is a fertile basin ringed by Aravalli hills. One history of the city notes that the surrounding terrain gave protection, because enemy cavalry and artillery could not move easily there. Lake Pichola already existed. It is believed to have been built in the fourteenth century, traditionally by a Banjara (trader) in 1362.

## The people

**Udai Singh II** became Maharana after a dangerous childhood and ruled from the late 1530s until his death in 1572.

**Banbir** was a relative who killed the reigning Rana, Vikramaditya (Udai Singh's half-brother), in the mid-1530s and seized power at Chittor.

**Panna Dhai** was Udai Singh's nurse. In the traditional account, she saved his life (see below).

**Asa Shah** was the governor of Kumbhalgarh, who sheltered the young prince.

**Akbar**, the Mughal emperor, besieged Chittorgarh in 1567–68.

**Jaimal Rathore of Badnore** and **Patta of Kelwa** were the commanders Udai Singh left to defend Chittor.

**Maharana Pratap** was Udai Singh's eldest son, who succeeded him.

## What happened

According to the traditional account, when Banbir came to kill the young Udai Singh, his nurse Panna had him carried out of Chittor hidden in a basket, and laid her own son in the prince's bed. Banbir killed that child. Panna brought Udai Singh to Kumbhalgarh, where he was raised in secret. He was crowned there, in 1540 by most accounts, and later recovered Chittor from Banbir.

As Maharana, Udai Singh began to develop the Girwa valley. The official Udaipur foundation history, published by the Maharana of Mewar Charitable Foundation, dates the founding of Udaipur to 1553. Other histories date the start of work to 1559, when the large lake named after him, Udai Sagar, was begun. Its dam is recorded as completed in 1565. The oldest part of the City Palace, the Rai Angan or royal courtyard, belongs to these early years. Later Maharanas kept adding to it for centuries.

Then came the crisis. In October 1567 Akbar arrived outside Chittorgarh. On the advice of his council, Udai Singh withdrew to the hills of western Mewar and left the fort in the hands of Jaimal and Patta. The siege lasted about four months. Jaimal was killed by a musket shot, which Mughal sources say Akbar fired himself. On the night of 22 February 1568, Rajput women inside the fort committed *jauhar*, choosing death by fire rather than capture. The fort fell on 23 February 1568, and the historian Satish Chandra describes the killing of about 30,000 people, defenders and peasants who had taken refuge there.

Chittor was lost, but Mewar was not conquered. Udai Singh held the western half of the kingdom. He died at Gogunda on 28 February 1572. His son Pratap would carry the resistance on, and Udaipur became the capital of Mewar.

## Legends and debates

**Panna Dhai.** Her sacrifice is one of Rajasthan's best-known stories. James Tod's 1829 *Annals* describes her as a Khichi Rajput woman named Panna, "the Diamond", and says the boy was carried out by a *bari* (a servant) in a basket covered with leaves. Later retellings name her son Chandan. The story is told consistently in later chronicles and histories, but I have not found a contemporary record of the events. It is best read as tradition with a historical core.

**The hermit on the hill.** The Mewar foundation history says that Udai Singh met a hermit, Goswami Prem Giri, on a hill above Pichola, who blessed him and told him to build his palace there. This is a founding legend.

**When was Udaipur founded?** You will see 1553 (the Mewar foundation), 1559 (many histories, and Britannica's City Palace article) and 1568 (Britannica's entry on the city, which says Udaipur was *made the capital* that year, after Chittor fell). These dates need not conflict: building may have begun in the 1550s, while the city became the capital only after 1568.

**1567 or 1568?** The siege began in 1567 and the fort fell in 1568, so both years appear.

**Coward or strategist?** Tod was scathing. He wrote that Udai Singh "had not one quality of a sovereign". Later writers often accused him of cowardice for leaving Chittor. The historian G. N. Sharma noted that he withdrew on his council's advice, and that no contemporary Persian author gave the impression of cowardice. Withdrawing to the hills was also exactly the strategy his son Pratap later used.

## Wisdom to carry forward

The city I loved most on this journey exists because a king left a fortress instead of dying in it. For a long time that choice was told as a failure. Without it, there might have been no Udaipur and no base from which Mewar could continue.

Retreat and defeat are not the same thing. Sometimes the bravest-looking choice and the wisest one are different, and it can take centuries for people to judge which was which.

## My connection

On Day 9 we reached Udaipur around 2 p.m. and checked into Hotel Udaigarh in the old-city area near Lake Pichola. The city immediately felt different to me. Compared with many stretches we had travelled through, I found it cleaner and neater, and it would eventually become my favourite city of the trip. That afternoon we visited Saheliyon Ki Bari and Fateh Sagar Lake. In the evening we went to Gangaur Ghat, with its views across Lake Pichola, and Bagore Ki Haveli.

On Day 10 our final full day began at the City Palace. It "grew over centuries under the rulers of Mewar", and I was drawn to the craftsmanship throughout: "the kind of work that makes you wonder how much patience and skill went into every surface." Udaipur gave me beauty, comfort, art and a sense of ease. That combination made it my favourite destination of the journey.
