From a distance, Kumbhalgarh looks less like a building than a line drawn across the Aravalli hills: wall after wall, rising and dipping with the ridges. It was built in the fifteenth century by Rana Kumbha of Mewar. For the next two hundred years it was the place Mewar's rulers turned to when everything else was in danger.

Its history follows the rest of Mewar's history closely. A hidden prince was raised here, and a famous ruler was born here. The king who built it was killed here too.

## Where and when

Kumbhalgarh stands in Rajsamand district, high in the Aravallis at about 1,100 metres. According to UNESCO's advisory body, ICOMOS, it controlled the key crossing between Mewar to the south-east and Marwar to the west. In 2013 it was inscribed on the World Heritage List as one of the six "Hill Forts of Rajasthan", alongside Chittorgarh, Ranthambore, Gagron, Amer and Jaisalmer.

Mewar was the kingdom of the Guhila dynasty and later of its Sisodia branch. Early Guhila inscriptions name Nagda and Ahar, both near present-day Udaipur, among their seats. Chittorgarh became the kingdom's great capital. After Delhi Sultanate armies took Chittor in 1303, the Sisodias, led by Hammir Singh, re-established the family's rule in the fourteenth century. Later, under pressure from the Mughals, the capital moved west into the hills, to Udaipur (and to Chavand for a time under Maharana Pratap). Kumbhalgarh was never the official capital. Rajasthan Tourism calls it Mewar's second most important citadel after Chittorgarh, and a refuge for its rulers in times of conflict.

## The people

**Rana Kumbha** came to the throne in 1433, aged about sixteen, after his father Rana Mokal was killed in a palace conspiracy. He ruled until 1468.

**Mandan** was his chief architect. ICOMOS notes that Kumbhalgarh's design is attributed to him by name, which is rare for buildings of this period. Mandan was also an author and theorist at Kumbha's court, and set out his ideas in a treatise, the *Rajavallabha*.

**Udai Singh I (Uda)**, Kumbha's son, is the one who killed him. He should not be confused with the later and better-known Udai Singh II, founder of Udaipur.

**Udai Singh II** and **Maharana Pratap** come later in the fort's story, as a child sheltered here and a son born here.

## What happened

Kumbha's reign was one of the high points of Mewar. He fought the sultans of Malwa and Gujarat. The Vijay Stambh, the "Tower of Victory" at Chittorgarh, commemorates his victory over Malwa, and Live History India dates it to 1448. He is credited with building or rebuilding a large number of forts across Mewar; one count gives 32. He was also a scholar and musician, to whom works on music and a commentary on the *Gita Govinda* are attributed. The great Jain temple at nearby Ranakpur was built during his reign, and an inscription of 1439 there names him.

Kumbhalgarh was his masterpiece of defence. Sources differ on the dates. ICOMOS says the outer walls were built between 1443 and 1458, over older wall structures. A Kumbhalgarh inscription is cited for 1448 as the year Kumbha ordered the fort's construction, and 1458 is often given as its completion. ICOMOS also notes that the fort was largely built "in a single process", which gives it unusual architectural unity. It lists seven gateways, Hindu and Jain temples, a fifteenth-century dam (the Badva Bund) and a stepwell (the Langan Baori) inside the walls. A palace was added much later by a Maharana named Fateh Singh.

In 1468 Kumbha was murdered at Kumbhalgarh, near the Mamadev temple, by his own son Uda.

About seventy years later, the fort played its most famous role. When the usurper Banbir seized Chittor in the late 1530s, the infant prince Udai Singh II was smuggled out and brought to Kumbhalgarh. James Tod records that its governor, Asa Shah, a Jain merchant, hid the boy by passing him off as his nephew. Udai Singh was crowned here, and his son Pratap is traditionally said to have been born here in 1540.

The fort was not invincible. In 1578, two years after Haldighati, Akbar's general Shahbaz Khan took it after a siege. Mewar later recovered it.

## Legends and debates

**How long are the walls?** Rajasthan Tourism says "some 36 kms", and that figure is repeated everywhere, sometimes with the claim that this is the world's second-longest wall. UNESCO's own evaluation document gives smaller and inconsistent figures: walls of "20km in length" in one place and a perimeter of "14km" in another. Claims about world rankings are popular rather than official, so treat them with caution.

**"Never captured"?** Guides often say the fort fell only once. ICOMOS says it was "only ever captured once and for a short period", in 1578. Other accounts describe it changing hands more than once in the following years.

**Protecting goddess.** Local belief holds that the goddess Banmata protected the fort from attack. This is a matter of faith rather than history.

**The rescue of Udai Singh.** The story of his nurse, Panna Dhai, who is said to have sacrificed her own son so that he could escape, comes from later chronicles and bardic tradition. It is told in the Udaipur story in this collection.

## Wisdom to carry forward

Kumbha built one of the strongest fortresses in India and was killed inside it by his own son. No wall, however long, protects against what happens within a family or a court.

Yet the same fort later did exactly what it was built for. It hid a child until he was safe. A defence is never complete, but it can still matter enormously at the moment it is needed.

## My connection

On Day 8 we travelled through the Aravalli landscape from Ranakpur to Kumbhalgarh. These two places were a contrast for me: a temple celebrated for its intricacy, and a fort celebrated for its immense defensive setting.

Kumbhalgarh is known for its extensive walls, often described as stretching for about 36 kilometres. It is also traditionally recognised as the birthplace of Maharana Pratap, whose stories, and Chetak's, I had grown up hearing. "It is one thing to hear those stories as a child, and another to travel through the region connected to them." The hills and fortifications made the scale of the landscape easier to imagine. We stayed overnight at Kumbhalgarh Junagarh Resort, and left early the next morning for Nathdwara, Eklingji and Udaipur.
