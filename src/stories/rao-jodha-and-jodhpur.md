From almost anywhere in Jodhpur's old city you can look up and see Mehrangarh. Its walls rise straight out of the rock, so it is hard to tell where the hill ends and the fort begins. Rudyard Kipling is often quoted as calling it the work of "angels and giants."

It was in fact begun in 1459 by a ruler who had spent years in exile winning back his inheritance, and who wanted a capital harder to take than the old one. Jodhpur's tradition adds two more figures: a hermit who did not want to leave, and a man remembered for giving his life to the foundations.

## Where and when

Jodhpur lies on the eastern edge of the Thar Desert, at the heart of the historic region of Marwar. Before 1459 the Rathore rulers of Marwar governed from Mandore, a few kilometres to the north. The Mehrangarh Museum Trust records that Rao Chunda (r. 1384–1428) acquired Mandore as part of a dowry and made it his capital.

Mandore was old and exposed. According to the Trust, Rao Jodha moved "to a new site six miles to the south, on an isolated rock," which offered height and natural defences. The hill rises about 400 feet above the plain (some sources say 410). Tradition calls it Bhaurcheeria, the "mountain of birds."

The Trust explains the name Mehrangarh as "fort of the sun," a reference to the Rathores' claimed descent from Surya, the sun god. According to their bards, the clan's ancestors had once ruled Kanauj, in present-day Uttar Pradesh, before moving west to Marwar.

## The people

**Rao Jodha** ruled Marwar from 1438 to 1489, according to the Mehrangarh Museum Trust. He was a son of **Rao Ranmal** (also called Ridmal).

**Rao Ranmal** was killed at Chittor in 1438 during a power struggle at the Mewar court. Accounts say Jodha escaped and spent years rebuilding his strength before recovering Mandore, usually dated to 1453.

Jodha had many sons. Among them were **Satal** and **Suja**, who succeeded him in turn at Jodhpur, and **Bika**, who founded Bikaner.

**Cheeria Nathji**, the hermit, **Rajiya Bambhi**, and **Karni Mata**, the Charan holy woman revered by the Rathores, belong to the founding legends discussed below.

## What happened

Once Jodha had recovered Mandore, he could think about a new capital. Britannica, the Mehrangarh Museum Trust and Incredible India all date Jodhpur's founding to 1459. The foundation of the fort is traditionally placed on 12 May 1459, and Jodhpur still marks that date as its founding day.

Jodha's fort was much smaller than the one visitors see today. Mehrangarh grew over the next four centuries. The Trust notes that Rao Maldeo (r. 1531–62) strengthened the gates and walls after the fort was captured by Sher Shah Sur's forces in 1544. Later rulers added palaces and gates. Fateh Pol commemorates a victory over the Mughals in 1707. Jai Pol was built by Maharaja Man Singh in 1806 after his victory over the armies of Jaipur and Bikaner, a reminder that Jodha's descendants in Jodhpur and Bikaner did not always stay friendly.

The city of Jodhpur grew in the fort's shadow and took its founder's name. The fort is still in the care of the former royal family. The Mehrangarh Museum Trust names the current head of the clan, Maharaja Gaj Singh II, as custodian of the fort, credited with preserving its buildings and developing its museum.

## Legends and debates

**The hermit's curse.** According to legend, the hill had one inhabitant, a hermit called Cheeria Nathji, the "lord of birds." When Jodha's men cleared the hill to build, the hermit was forced out. In anger he cursed the place with scarcity of water. One version says Jodha turned to Karni Mata, who persuaded the hermit to leave. Another says the king's men evicted him and the new walls collapsed. In most tellings Jodha tried to appease him by building him a dwelling and a temple within the fort.

**Rajiya Bambhi.** According to tradition, a man named Rajiya Bambhi (also recorded as Rajiya Bhambi and as Rajaram Meghwal) volunteered to be buried alive in the fort's foundations to bring it good fortune. He did so on the condition that the ruling family would look after his descendants. Some retellings say his sacrifice was needed to break the hermit's curse. Others treat the two stories separately. Accounts say his family was granted land in an area of Jodhpur called Raj Bagh, where his descendants still live. A memorial to him has been pointed out to visitors inside the fort. We cannot know what really happened in 1459, but the city has kept his name alive for centuries.

**Karni Mata's foundation.** Devotional accounts of Karni Mata say she laid the foundation of Jodhpur's fort in 1459 and foretold that Jodha's descendants would rule it for many generations. This is a matter of faith.

**Dates and spellings.** Jodha's dates are usually given as 1416–1489, and his accession as 1438 or 1439. The hill appears as Bhaurcheeria, Bhakurcheeria or Chidiya-tunk, and the hermit sometimes as Chidiya Nath.

**The "angels and giants" quote.** It is widely attributed to Kipling, but I could not trace it to a specific published work.

## Wisdom to carry forward

Mehrangarh was built to impress, and it still does. Its own founding legend, though, puts an ordinary man at the bottom of the walls, not a king or a god. Whether or not Rajiya Bambhi was really buried there, Jodhpur chose to remember the bargain: a life given, and a family to be looked after in return.

That is worth noticing in a place so full of royal portraits. A city's story is also kept by the people who insist on remembering the names that would otherwise be forgotten.

## My connection

We reached Jodhpur on the seventh day, after our night in the Khuri desert. Mehrangarh dominates the skyline, and its towering walls and elevated position create a sense of scale that I found difficult to capture in a photograph. Inside, the palace areas and museum collections revealed the ceremonial and artistic world of Marwar's rulers. The views over Jodhpur were impressive, but I felt the interiors deserved just as much attention. As I wrote in my diary, "Rajasthan's forts were never only about defence; they were also repositories of craft and memory."

We also visited Jaswant Thada, Umaid Bhawan Palace, the Clock Tower and Sardar Market, and stayed at Rohet Garh, my favourite hotel of the trip. Jodhpur reinforced something I had noticed throughout Rajasthan: the extraordinary effort that has gone into preserving its royal buildings and cultural forms. All the places we visited in Jodhpur were enjoyable. The day was simply a very good one.
