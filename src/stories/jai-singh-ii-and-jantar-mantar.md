Walk a few minutes from Jaipur's City Palace and you reach a courtyard that looks at first like a sculpture park: a giant triangular wall with a staircase running up its spine, sunken marble bowls, curving ramps and round towers open to the sky. Nothing here is decorative. Each structure is an instrument, built from stone and masonry, for measuring the movements of the sun, moon and stars.

The man behind it, Sawai Jai Singh II, is usually remembered as the founder of Jaipur. But he was also a serious student of astronomy, and the Jantar Mantar is the place where those two sides of him meet. He planned a new capital and, right beside his palace, a place to check the sky against the books.

## Where and when

Jai Singh II ruled the Kachwaha Rajput kingdom of Amber (Amer), which was a feudatory state of the Mughal Empire. Different sources put his birth in 1686 or 1688, and his accession in 1699 or 1700. He reigned until his death in 1743.

His lifetime covered the years when Mughal power started to break apart after Aurangzeb died in 1707. Jai Singh nevertheless held on to his influence at the imperial court. According to the British Museum's biography, Aurangzeb gave him the title "Sawai", meaning "one and a quarter", after he captured the fort of Vishalgarh from the Marathas in 1701. Emperor Muhammad Shah, who came to the throne in 1719, also kept him in favour.

The Jantar Mantar in Jaipur was built in the first half of the eighteenth century. UNESCO describes it as the most significant, most comprehensive and best preserved of India's historic observatories, with about twenty main fixed instruments. It was inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2010. The walled city around it was inscribed separately in 2019.

## The people

**Sawai Jai Singh II** is the central figure. The British Museum describes him as a ruler who was also a mathematician, an astronomer and a town planner. He collected astronomical manuscripts and tables from Central Asia, the Islamic world and Europe, including the work of Ptolemy, Ulugh Beg of Samarkand, John Flamsteed and Philippe de La Hire.

**Muhammad Shah**, the Mughal emperor, backed the observatory project with imperial support. Jai Singh's astronomical tables were named after him.

**Vidyadhar** is named by UNESCO as the architect-planner of Jaipur. He laid the city out on a grid inspired by the *prastara* plan of the Vastu Shastra, the traditional Hindu treatise on architecture.

**Jagannatha**, a scholar, is described by some writers as Jai Singh's chief adviser on astronomy. I found this in secondary sources only (see notes).

## What happened

According to the British Museum, Jai Singh raised a practical problem with the emperor. The astronomical tables then in use gave planetary positions that did not match what was actually seen in the sky, and that affected when Hindu and Muslim religious observances were held. His answer was to observe the sky again, more precisely, using instruments far bigger than usual. A bigger instrument has more room for finely graduated scales, so in principle it can give finer readings.

With imperial backing he built five observatories, all now called Jantar Mantar: at Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi and Mathura. Delhi's is usually dated to 1724 and is generally described as the first. Four of the five still stand. The Mathura observatory has been lost, and one account says it was dismantled in the nineteenth century. Spreading the sites out had a scientific use, because readings taken at several locations could be compared. The places he chose also mattered politically and culturally. Delhi was the imperial seat, Ujjain lay on an old Indian prime meridian and Varanasi was a long-established centre of learning.

The Jaipur observatory became the largest. Its best-known instrument is the Vrihat Samrat Yantra, a huge equinoctial sundial about 27 metres tall, with a gnomon (the triangular wall that casts the shadow) set parallel to the Earth's axis. The Jai Prakash Yantra is a pair of sunken hemispherical bowls in which the position of the sun can be read against markings. Other instruments measured altitude and azimuth or tracked the sun's passage across the celestial equator.

Meanwhile Jai Singh was building a city. Jaipur was laid out in the late 1720s as a planned capital about 11 kilometres from the old fort-palace at Amer. Its grid of blocks, broad bazaars and city walls were designed in a single phase. The observatory sits inside the palace precinct, close to the City Palace, as part of the same plan.

The observations fed into a set of astronomical tables, the *Zij-i Muhammad Shahi*, dedicated to the emperor. The British Museum dates its completion to 1728. UNESCO says observations at Jantar Mantar contributed to completing these tables.

The site's later history was less grand. UNESCO notes that it was temporarily abandoned in the nineteenth century and then restored several times over more than a century. Restorations are recorded under Sawai Ram Singh II (1876) and again around 1901. So some of what you see today, including the plaster and some of the scale markings, has been renewed.

## Legends and debates

**When was it built?** Sources do not agree. The Ministry of Tourism's site gives 1728–1734, a BBC Sky at Night article says the site was completed in 1734, one researcher's site gives about 1738, and another popular history gives 1724–1727. UNESCO itself says only "the first half of the eighteenth century". The founding of Jaipur is similarly given as 1726, 1727 or 1728. That depends partly on whether a source means the start of building or the formal founding: 18 November 1727 is the date most often quoted.

**"Accurate to two seconds."** The Samrat Yantra is very often described as the world's largest sundial, accurate to about two seconds. Its scale is divided finely enough to suggest that. But the sun's shadow has a soft, blurred edge, which (as Wikipedia's article points out) makes such precision hard to achieve in practice. Treat the "two seconds" as a measure of the scale's fineness, not a guarantee.

**The quarter flag.** Tradition holds that because of the "Sawai" title (one and a quarter), Jaipur's rulers flew two flags, one full-sized and one quarter-sized. The British Museum repeats this, but it is the kind of story that is easier to repeat than to date.

**The name.** "Jantar Mantar" is generally explained as coming from *yantra* (instrument) and *mantra* (here meaning calculation or formula). It is not a formal title.

## Wisdom to carry forward

Jai Singh had access to the most respected astronomical tables of his day, from Samarkand, Europe and the Indian tradition. He did not simply choose the most prestigious one. He noticed that the books and the sky disagreed, and he trusted the sky enough to build a way of checking.

His instruments did not end up advancing science the way telescopes did, and they needed constant repair. Still, the habit behind them is worth keeping: when what you were taught does not match what you can see, measure it yourself, as carefully as you can.

## My connection

On our first day in Jaipur, after the City Palace, we went to Jantar Mantar. I wrote in my diary that it "offered a very different kind of wonder. Here, enormous instruments of stone and masonry were built to observe the heavens and measure time." It reminded me that the ruler who founded Jaipur was deeply interested in astronomy.

I liked all four places we saw that day, City Palace, Jantar Mantar, Hawa Mahal and Jal Mahal, and I did not want to pick a winner. What I enjoyed was how different they were. The next evening, from the hills at Nahargarh, I could see the planned city as a whole for the first time. Walking through a street lined with terracotta buildings is one thing. Looking down on the city from above is quite another.
