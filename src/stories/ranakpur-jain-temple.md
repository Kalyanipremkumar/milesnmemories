You come upon Ranakpur almost by surprise. The road winds through the green folds of the Aravalli hills, and then a great pale temple appears in a quiet valley, its spires and domes climbing up the slope. Inside, the light falls between rows and rows of carved marble pillars, and the building seems to go on and on.

This is the Chaturmukha Dharana Vihara, the main Jain temple at Ranakpur. Its name holds two clues to its story: *chaturmukha*, "four-faced", describes its design, and *Dharana* recalls the man who paid for it. Behind it stand three people we can actually name from the stone itself: a Jain merchant, an architect, and a king.

## Where and when

Ranakpur lies near the town of Sadri in Pali district, on the western side of the Aravallis, in the old kingdom of Mewar. The temple belongs to the Shvetambara Jain tradition and is dedicated to Adinatha (also called Rishabhanatha), the first of the 24 Tirthankaras, the great teachers of Jainism. It is counted among the most important Jain pilgrimage sites in India.

The firmest date we have comes from a Sanskrit inscription of 47 lines carved on a pillar near the entrance. It is dated 1439 CE (Vikram Samvat 1496), in the reign of Rana Kumbha, who ruled Mewar from 1433 to 1468. Work clearly continued for many years after that: sources give different end dates, and Cornell University's architecture collection simply places the building between about 1440 and 1500.

The temple is built in the Maru-Gurjara style of western India and is enormous, roughly 60 by 62 metres, about the size of a Gothic cathedral.

## The people

**Dharna Shah** (also written Dharanaka or Dhanna Shah) was a Jain merchant of the Porwal community. Several accounts describe him as a minister at Rana Kumbha's court. The inscription names him as the builder, the person who commissioned the temple.

**Depaka** (also Depa or Deepaka) is named in the same inscription as the architect. Temple tradition says he came from a nearby village and that his plan won Dharna Shah over after better-known designers had failed.

**Rana Kumbha** was the Sisodia ruler of Mewar, famous for his forts, including nearby Kumbhalgarh. He granted the land, and the town's name, Ranakpur, is generally linked to him. The inscription praises Kumbha and a long line of his predecessors and says the temple was built "by his grace and on his orders", which shows how a Hindu king and a Jain merchant could share credit for a sacred building.

**Acharya Somasundara Suri**, a leading monk of the Tapa Gaccha, is named as the person who consecrated the temple. He is also described as Dharna Shah's spiritual guide.

## What happened

The heart of the design is the *chaturmukha*, or four-faced, plan. In the central sanctum sits a white marble image of Adinatha with four faces, one looking out of each of four doorways. Each doorway opens onto halls, which open onto further halls, so a worshipper can approach the Tirthankara from any direction. The four faces are usually read as a symbol of the Tirthankara's message reaching all four directions of the world.

Around this centre the builders arranged dozens of halls, small domed pavilions and a ring of subsidiary shrines (devakulikas), with five spires rising above. Because the halls are open on so many sides, light and air move through the whole building, and as you walk the view keeps changing between columns.

What most visitors remember is the carving. The ceilings are worked into concentric rings and lace-like pendants. Pillars carry dancers, musicians, elephants and lions. One celebrated panel shows Parshvanatha, the 23rd Tirthankara, sheltered by a serpent with a canopy of many hoods. It is often said to have 1,008 snake heads, carved from a single slab.

The temple did not always flourish. Accounts describe a long decline: the surrounding population thinned, and the site was damaged and neglected. In the twentieth century the Anandji Kalyanji Pedhi, the Jain trust that manages it, carried out a major restoration. Sources give different dates for this work, from the early 1900s to a reopening in 1953.

## Legends and debates

**The dream.** According to tradition, Dharna Shah dreamt of a *Nalinigulma vimana*, a celestial palace, and wanted the temple to look like it. This is a cherished story, not a documented event.

**The 1,444 pillars.** The number is repeated everywhere, but it is best understood as the *traditional* figure. Published counts differ widely: one widely used reference gives 1,444, while others list about 420 or 426 columns. It is not clear how the traditional number was reached, for example whether it counts pilasters and small supports. A popular saying adds that the pillars cannot be counted at all. What every source agrees on is that the carving varies from pillar to pillar.

**The starting date.** Some accounts say construction began in 1389. That is hard to fit with the inscription, which credits Rana Kumbha, who did not come to the throne until 1433. The inscription of 1439 is the safest anchor.

**The unfinished pillar.** Tradition holds that one pillar was left deliberately incomplete, or that it fell each night as it was built. This is folklore, often told as a lesson about humility.

## Wisdom to carry forward

At Ranakpur the person whose name matters most, the architect, survives mostly as a single word in an inscription. Thousands of carvers left no names at all. The building reminds us that great works are almost always shared: a merchant's money, a king's land, a monk's blessing, and the patience of many unnamed hands.

It is also a lesson in repetition. A row of pillars looks identical from a distance and turns out to be full of difference up close. Some kinds of beauty only show themselves to someone willing to slow down.

## My connection

We visited Ranakpur on 17 October, on the way through the Aravalli landscape towards Kumbhalgarh. By then I had seen many kinds of Rajasthani architecture, and Ranakpur offered a contrast: a temple celebrated for its intricacy.

What fascinated me was the discipline behind the work: so much repetition, yet so much variation. As I wrote that day, "Stone is a hard material, but in skilled hands it can seem delicate and light." For someone who loves painting and pattern, Ranakpur offered a different lesson. Lines I might draw with a pen or brush had been translated into carved stone. The details invite patience; there is far more to see than a quick glance can absorb.
